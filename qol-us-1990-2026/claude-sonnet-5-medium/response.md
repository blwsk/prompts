# U.S. Quality of Life, 1990–2026: A Breakdown by Income, Generation, and Geography

*A synthesis of the best available data, organized by factor. Where evidence is contested, that's flagged explicitly.*

---

## 1. Financial: Income, Wealth, Debt

**Income growth has been real but sharply unequal.** Median household income has more than doubled since the late 1940s and continues to reach record highs in inflation-adjusted terms as of 2024–25. But the *distribution* of that growth is the real story: the Gini coefficient rose from 0.43 in 1990 to 0.49 in 2024, and cumulative real income growth since the 1970s shows the top quintile (and especially the top 5%) pulling steadily away from the bottom four quintiles, a divergence that accelerated in the mid-1980s. Looking at dual-earner households specifically, one recent study found median household earnings up ~30% from 1976–2022, but the bottom quartile grew only ~19% (and was flat from 1976–2000), while the 90th percentile grew ~77% and the 95th nearly doubled.

- **By quintile:** Bottom quintile income is essentially flat since 2019 in real terms; top quintiles are at all-time highs. Wealth concentration is even starker than income concentration — the top 10% now holds roughly two-thirds of household net worth, a share that has grown steadily since 1990.
- **By generation:** Millennials and Gen Z show similar or slightly better *income* trajectories than prior generations at the same age (per St. Louis Fed research), but materially worse *wealth* accumulation once student debt is netted out — Gen X and Millennial 30-year-olds carry substantially more debt (especially student loans) than Boomers did at 30, even though gross wealth looks similar.
- **By geography:** Income and wealth gains have concentrated in large metros with strong knowledge-economy sectors; incomes in many rural counties and small manufacturing metros have stagnated or grown much more slowly since 1990.
- **Contested/thin:** Whether "wealth" comparisons across generations are apples-to-apples is debated — cohort size, timing of asset purchases relative to market cycles (Boomers buying stocks/homes before major bull markets), and family transfers (inheritances, parental down-payment help) all complicate clean generational comparisons.

## 2. Cost of Living: Housing, Food, Education, Healthcare, Childcare

This is where the "richer on paper, squeezed in practice" story concentrates. Overall CPI rose roughly 130–135% from 1990 to 2024, but the categories that matter most for life-stage costs rose far faster:

- **Childcare:** Day care/preschool costs rose **263%** between 1990 and 2024 — about double overall CPI (KPMG/BLS data). A cost once considered a 7%-of-income benchmark for affordability now routinely runs 10–20%+ of household income for young families.
- **Education:** Tuition/fees and childcare-related CPI outpaced overall inflation consistently from 2000 through the mid-2010s, per St. Louis Fed analysis of BLS series, though the gap has narrowed somewhat since 2017 as tuition growth has cooled.
- **Housing:** The single largest driver of "it feels harder even though incomes are up." (Detailed below.)
- **Healthcare:** Medical care CPI has also outpaced headline CPI over most of this period, though the gap has been narrower in the 2010s–2020s than in the 1990s–2000s as cost-growth deceleration set in.
- **Food:** Broadly tracked CPI overall, with sharp spikes in 2021–2023, but hasn't diverged as dramatically as housing/childcare/education over the full 1990–2024 window.

**By quintile:** These fixed, hard-to-avoid costs (housing, childcare, healthcare) consume a much larger *share* of income for the bottom two quintiles, meaning the same dollar figure represents a far heavier burden than it did in 1990 — even where nominal/real income has risen modestly.

**By generation:** Millennials and Gen Z are absorbing the childcare/housing/education cost spike during exactly the life stage (family formation, home-buying, early career) when prior generations faced much gentler versions of these same costs. Boomers largely built families and bought homes before this multi-decade acceleration.

**By geography:** Urban/coastal metro cost-of-living increases (especially housing) have outpaced suburban and rural areas by a wide margin, though rural areas have seen faster-than-historical-average healthcare and childcare cost growth too, often against a thinner base of local service providers.

## 3. Housing: Availability, Ownership, Rent Burden

This is probably the most-changed dimension of American life since 1990, and the data are unusually clear:

- **Homeownership by generation, controlled for age:** 27-year-olds in 2024 owned homes at 33% vs. 40% for Boomers at 27; 35-year-olds owned at 56% vs. 61.5% for Boomers at the same age (Redfin/Census analysis). Millennials at 30 owned homes 15 percentage points below Boomers at 30 (Berkeley Institute for Young Americans). At the person level, Gen Z ownership sits near 9–27% depending on cutoff age, well below where Boomers and even Gen X were tracking at comparable ages.
- **Structural causes:** Mortgage rates jumped from ~3% (2021) to 6–7% (2022–2025); home prices rose 53% since 2020 alone; supply is constrained partly because Boomers are aging in place rather than selling (a documented "lock-in" effect); construction shifted post-2008 away from suburban single-family toward urban multifamily, worsening starter-home scarcity.
- **By quintile:** Homeownership decline is concentrated among lower-income, non-college, and (per Apartment List research) even some higher-income young households who are delaying purchase; the built-for-rent single-family sector has boomed as a workaround, effectively converting what used to be an ownership stake into a permanent rental relationship for many middle-income millennials/Gen Z.
- **By geography:** Rural and small-metro housing remains comparatively affordable but often lacks the job base to make that affordability meaningful; suburban housing (long the default aspirational middle-class asset) has appreciated fastest; urban rents have outpaced urban home-price growth somewhat, making renting relatively more viable than buying in cities.
- **Contested:** Whether declining homeownership reflects *preference* (delayed marriage/childbearing, valuing flexibility) versus *constraint* (affordability) is genuinely disputed — Redfin's own analysts and demographic researchers cite both, and untangling "age effect" from "cohort effect" from "one-time economic shock effect" is a live methodological problem even for the researchers who study it (see St. Louis Fed, Berkeley).

## 4. Health & Longevity

A genuinely mixed and swing-y picture, not a straight line:

- **Life expectancy:** Rose from the mid-70s in 1990 to a peak around 78.8 (2019), then fell sharply in 2020–2021 (COVID), and has since recovered to **79.0 years in 2024** — a new high, driven substantially by the largest-ever one-year drop in drug overdose deaths (26% decline 2023→2024).
- **"Deaths of despair"** (drug, alcohol, suicide) rose from a crude rate of 28.6/100,000 in 1999 to 72/100,000 in 2023 — a genuine, well-documented deterioration concentrated among non-college whites and working-age adults (25–64), particularly acute from 2014–2021, with encouraging (but recent and unproven-to-last) declines in overdose deaths in 2023–2024.
- **International comparison:** A comparative mortality study (1990–2019) found the U.S. uniquely worsening across a wide range of midlife causes (drug, alcohol, suicide, metabolic, respiratory) relative to peer high-income countries and even relative to former Eastern Bloc nations recovering from their own 1990s mortality crisis.
- **By geography:** Rural/non-metro mortality has diverged upward from urban mortality since 1990 specifically — a documented, sustained "rural mortality penalty" that didn't exist as starkly before 1990.
- **By generation:** Gen X and older Millennials are the cohorts most exposed to the "deaths of despair" excess mortality (25–64 age band, concentrated in 1999–2023). Gen Z shows some encouraging recent signs (falling teen substance use in many categories) alongside the mental-health concerns below.
- **Contested:** How much of the 2020s life-expectancy recovery is a durable trend vs. a mechanical rebound from COVID and a temporary opioid-supply shift is unresolved; researchers flag 2024 data as still provisional.

## 5. Mental Health, Attention, and Social Fabric

- **Teen mental health:** A real and sharply-timed inflection around 2011–2012 (not COVID) — depression, self-harm, and loneliness metrics all begin rising then, coincident with smartphone/social-media saturation (Haidt/Twenge). Teen suicide rates are reported up over 50% since roughly 2010.
- **The causal debate is genuinely live, not settled**, despite how it's often presented in media. Haidt and Twenge argue for a strong causal role of smartphones/social media, citing dose-response correlations (heavy, 4+ hour/day use shows much steeper associations) and cross-national timing. Critics (Odgers, Orben & Przybylski, and others) point to weak effect sizes in randomized/quasi-experimental studies and argue for a more modest, contributing-factor role alongside other explanations (declining unstructured play, school pressure, economic anxiety). Treat any single-cause narrative here with real skepticism — this is one of the more contested empirical areas in this whole analysis.
- **Family formation:** Marriage rates have declined substantially and consistently since 1990; first marriage and first birth now happen markedly later (average first-time-mother age rose from 24.9 to 27.5 over roughly two decades). This is partly *preference* (career-building, cohabitation as a substitute) and partly *constraint* (linked to housing affordability and debt, per the St. Louis Fed generational research above).
- **Institutional trust:** Declined steadily since the late 1970s per Gallup/Pew, with a faster drop in recent years; the U.S. now trusts institutions less than many peer nations. Interpersonal (person-to-person) trust, by contrast, is fairly flat and doesn't vary dramatically by urban/suburban/rural geography (31–37% "most people can be trusted" across the urbanization spectrum) — it varies far more by birth cohort (each cohort born since the 1990s trusts less than the one before) and by income/education than by geography.
- **By quintile:** Lower-income and non-college respondents report systematically lower trust in hospitals, police, and public institutions than higher-income/college-educated peers — a persistent, not narrowing, gap.
- **Contested/thin:** Whether declining marriage/trust represents a "loss" or a "recomposition" of social life (e.g., more chosen community, more geographic mobility, different but not necessarily thinner social ties) is a values-laden interpretive question, not just an empirical one.

## 6. Technology: Productivity, Convenience, Downsides

- **Upside:** Genuine, large gains in convenience, information access, communication cost (near-zero long-distance/video calling vs. real cost in 1990), retail logistics, medical diagnostics, and — for knowledge workers — flexibility (remote work, unavailable at scale before ~2020). Consumer electronics and many goods have gotten dramatically cheaper in real terms even as services (housing, healthcare, education, childcare) got more expensive — this "goods vs. services" divergence is one of the defining, underappreciated stories of the whole 1990–2026 period.
- **Downside:** Documented associations between heavy device/social-media use and mental health/attention outcomes (see above); labor displacement risk is real but its *realized* impact through 2026 has been more gradual and sector-specific (customer service, some clerical/administrative work, translation, some coding tasks) than the most dramatic forecasts suggested — this is an area where forward-looking claims (especially about AI) are more speculative than backward-looking data.
- **By generation:** Boomers and Gen X adopted these tools as adults with fully-formed social patterns; Millennials transitioned during early adulthood; Gen Z is the first cohort whose adolescence was fully saturated by smartphones and algorithmic social media — which is precisely the cohort showing the sharpest mental-health deterioration, though (per above) causal attribution remains contested.
- **By geography:** Broadband/tech access gaps between urban and rural areas have narrowed substantially since 1990 (though rural gaps in fiber/high-speed access persist), while remote work has, since 2020, been a rare technology-driven force *helping* geographic cost-of-living arbitrage — letting some high-income remote workers relocate to lower-cost suburban/rural areas, a genuinely new and still-unfolding dynamic.

## Summary Verdict, By Group

**By income quintile**
- *Top quintile:* Better, and by a wide margin — record real incomes, disproportionate wealth gains, housing affordability barely dented by rate/price increases given cash/equity positions.
- *Middle quintiles:* Mixed-to-slightly-worse — modest real income gains eaten by outsized growth in housing, childcare, and healthcare as a share of budget; homeownership and family formation delayed but usually still achievable.
- *Bottom quintile:* Worse on the cost-of-living and housing fronts specifically, roughly flat-to-slightly-better on raw income (helped by 1990s–2020s minimum wage and EITC changes in many states) — but real income is still below its 2019 peak as of 2024, and the "hard costs" (rent, childcare, health) consume a larger income share than in 1990.

**By generation**
- *Boomers:* Better, clearly — bought homes and built careers before the multi-decade housing/childcare/education cost acceleration; now hold the largest generational share of both real estate and financial wealth; downside is health-related (this cohort bears much of the "deaths of despair" excess mortality in midlife, and is now navigating an aging population's healthcare-cost exposure).
- *Gen X:* Mixed — caught the tail end of the pre-2008 housing market, recovered wealth more slowly post-crisis than any other generation, carries more debt at each life stage than Boomers did, but has now reached solid homeownership rates (72.9%) and career peak earnings.
- *Millennials:* Mixed, trending worse on wealth-building specifically — homeownership and family formation delayed by 5-10+ years relative to Boomers at the same age; the "delayed, not denied" framing may prove correct for the median millennial, but the cohort has absorbed the worst of both the 2008 crisis (early career) and the 2020s housing/rate spike (peak home-buying years).
- *Gen Z:* Too early to fully verdict, but early indicators are mixed: some encouraging signs (slightly better income/homeownership at the same age than millennials in some measures, thanks partly to remote work and family financial support), some concerning ones (adolescent mental health, latest to enter an unaffordable housing market, highest exposure to AI-driven labor market uncertainty during early career).

**By geography**
- *Urban:* Mixed — best access to high-income jobs and amenities, but bears the steepest housing cost increases and, for renters specifically, the worst rent burden.
- *Suburban:* Best-positioned overall — still the default aspirational setting for family formation, benefiting from remote-work-enabled relocation, though increasingly unaffordable at the entry level for young/first-time buyers.
- *Rural:* Worse on the clearest metrics — a real, sustained rural mortality penalty since 1990, slower income growth, thinner healthcare/childcare infrastructure, and the lowest institutional trust — offset only partly by lower absolute cost of living and (per Pew) roughly comparable interpersonal trust levels to urban areas.

## The Biggest Tensions

1. **Goods got cheaper; the things that structure a life got much more expensive.** Consumer electronics, clothing, and many groceries are real bargains relative to 1990. Housing, childcare, healthcare, and education — the four costs tied to actually building a household — rose far faster than income for most people. This is arguably the single most important structural fact in this whole analysis: it explains why "the data" (rising real income, rising GDP, more consumer stuff) and "the vibes" (housing feels impossible, family formation feels unaffordable) can both be true at once.

2. **Richer in aggregate, more unequal and more anxious in distribution.** Real income, wealth, and life expectancy are all at or near record highs at the national level — but the *distribution* of those gains (across quintiles, and now increasingly across generations) has become more concentrated, and the "losers" in relative terms (bottom quintile, non-college workers, rural residents, Gen X/Millennial wealth-builders) are not a small or marginal group.

3. **More connected, more isolated.** Technology delivered real convenience and communication gains, but arrived alongside — and plausibly, though not conclusively, contributed to — declining institutional trust, delayed/reduced family formation, and (especially for the generation that grew up with it) a documented mental-health deterioration whose causal story is still being argued over by serious researchers on both sides.

## Where I'm Uncertain or Biased

- **I weighted "hard-to-avoid" costs (housing, childcare, healthcare) more heavily than headline income growth** in forming these verdicts, because I think a rising median income that's increasingly consumed by fixed costs represents less real gain in lived experience than the raw number suggests. Someone who weights headline real-income and consumer-goods-price data more heavily would reasonably reach a more optimistic verdict, especially for the middle quintiles.
- **The social-media/mental-health section is the one place I flagged genuine expert disagreement**, and I tried not to adjudicate it — but my framing of "the timing is striking" still reflects a view (shared by Haidt/Twenge but contested by other researchers) that correlation-plus-timing is more probative than critics believe.
- **Generational verdicts compress enormous internal variation.** "Millennials" spans people born 1981–1996 who range from priced-out renters to first-generation-wealth homeowners; any generational verdict is a statement about medians and trends, not about any individual's experience.
- **I did not have strong recent (2025–2026) data on some threads** — the durability of the 2023–2024 overdose-death decline, the labor-market impact of AI/automation, and the very latest housing-affordability numbers are all still unfolding, and I've flagged those as provisional rather than settled.
