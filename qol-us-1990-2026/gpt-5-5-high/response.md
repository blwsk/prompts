Bottom line

From 1990 to the present, U.S. quality of life has become more unequal and more conditional. For upper-income households, homeowners, older asset-holders, and many suburban professionals, life is materially better: higher real incomes, much greater wealth, better technology, safer and more convenient consumption, and more medical capability. For lower-income renters, younger non-homeowners, many rural communities, and families paying market prices for housing, childcare, education, or healthcare, the story is much more mixed and often worse.

The strongest pattern is this: America became richer, more technologically capable, and more convenient, but the core “adult life package” — stable housing, family formation, healthcare, childcare, education, and community — became harder to secure for many people.

1. By income quintile

Group	Verdict since 1990	What improved	What worsened
Bottom 20%	Mixed to worse	Transfers, Medicaid/CHIP, refundable credits, cheaper consumer tech, some recent low-wage gains	Rent burden, low wealth, chronic disease, unstable work, debt stress, weak mobility
Second 20%	Mixed / squeezed	Real income modestly higher, better goods/technology, some wage gains after 2015	Housing, childcare, healthcare, transportation, and education costs absorb gains
Middle 20%	Mixed	Higher real household income, better homes/goods/tech, more dual-earner earnings	Homeownership harder for new entrants, less wealth security, family costs up
Fourth 20%	Moderately better	Stronger incomes, home equity, retirement assets, remote-work upside	College/childcare/housing stress, especially in high-cost metros
Top 20%	Clearly better, with social caveats	Large income and wealth gains, asset appreciation, better healthcare access, tech leverage	More inequality around them, social fragmentation, work intensity, attention costs

CBO’s long-run distribution data show the key income pattern: real household income grew across the distribution, but it grew most at the top, raising inequality. CBO also finds that after-tax-and-transfer income is more equal than market income because transfers and tax credits became more important, especially for the bottom quintile. Medicaid/CHIP benefits for the lowest quintile rose sharply as a share of income, and refundable credits also became more important.  

Wealth is even more unequal than income. Federal Reserve distributional data show that the top 10% of households by wealth held about two-thirds of household wealth, while the bottom 50% held only a small share. By income, the top income quintile held over 70% of wealth, while the bottom quintile held around 3%.  

For the bottom two quintiles, the quality-of-life picture is not simply “poorer than 1990.” They have better electronics, safer cars, broader public health coverage, more information access, and in some periods stronger wage gains. But housing and healthcare exposure dominate. The Joint Center for Housing Studies reports record renter cost burdens, and renters with incomes below $30,000 have especially high burden rates.  

For the top quintile, the main caveat is that “quality of life” is not just money. Wealth, convenience, and optionality improved dramatically, but trust, family formation, attention, and mental health trends look worse or ambiguous.

2. By generation

Generation	Verdict	Core story
Boomers	Mostly better, especially homeowners	Bought homes and accumulated assets before the worst affordability squeeze; now face healthcare, aging, and isolation risks
Gen X	Mixed-positive, highly path-dependent	Hit by dot-com and Great Recession shocks, but many recovered through home equity and retirement accounts
Millennials	Mixed / bifurcated	Started adulthood during the Great Recession, delayed family/housing, high student debt; recent wealth gains helped homeowners and investors
Gen Z	Mixed, leaning strained for median young adults	Better technology and some strong early labor-market conditions, but very high housing costs, social-media-era mental health concerns, and delayed independence

Boomers benefited from entering adulthood before today’s housing affordability constraints and before college and childcare costs rose as much relative to young-adult incomes. Many also rode the long boom in housing, equities, and retirement accounts. Their present-day risks are different: healthcare costs, long-term care, loneliness, and retirement adequacy for non-asset-holders.

Gen X is the “volatility generation.” Federal Reserve research comparing generations at age 30 found Gen X and Millennials had much higher debt than Boomers at the same age, with especially higher mortgage and education debt. Gen X also carried the scars of the Great Recession during peak household-formation years.  

Millennials were behind earlier in adulthood, but the story changed after 2019. St. Louis Fed analysis finds older Millennials’ median wealth moved from well below expectations in 2016 to above expectations by 2022; younger Millennials/older Gen Z also saw large gains. Still, this improvement is uneven and depends heavily on owning assets before the post-2020 housing and stock-market run-up.  

For young adults overall, housing and family formation are clearly harder than in 1990. Urban Institute data show young-adult homeownership fell from 45.0% in 1990 to 37.0% in 2015, then recovered to 41.6% in 2021; young adults living with parents rose from 12.5% to 20.2%, and marriage among young adults fell sharply.  

Gen Z has the least complete evidence because many are not yet fully through household formation. They have strong digital tools and, recently, a relatively tight labor market for young workers. But their baseline costs for rent, homes, education credentials, and mental health are unfavorable. CDC youth data show high levels of persistent sadness/hopelessness and suicidal ideation among high school students in 2023, and adolescent diagnosed anxiety and depression rose notably from 2016 to 2023.  

3. By geography

Geography	Verdict	Core story
Urban	Better for high earners; worse for many renters	More jobs, amenities, medicine, education, and diversity; severe housing costs and social fragmentation
Suburban	Mostly better for homeowners	Home equity, schools, safety, space, remote work; but car dependence, exclusionary zoning, and high entry prices
Rural	Mixed to worse	Lower nominal housing costs and stronger local ties in some places; weaker job markets, worse health outcomes, lower access, aging population

Urban America gained from high-productivity service sectors, universities, hospitals, restaurants, culture, and agglomeration. But housing supply failed to keep up in many productive metros. JCHS highlights soaring home-price-to-income ratios, record renter burdens, and rising homelessness as major housing symptoms.  

Suburban households that already owned homes often saw large gains: larger homes, rising home equity, better schools, and wealth accumulation. New entrants face the reverse: high prices, high mortgage rates, long commutes, and childcare-heavy family budgets.

Rural America is not uniformly worse; some rural places have lower housing costs, stronger place attachment, and less congestion. But the aggregate evidence is unfavorable on income, demographics, labor-force participation, and health. FHFA notes that rural areas are older, have lower labor-force participation, and have a larger share of households under $50,000 income than non-rural areas. CDC data show the rural mortality disadvantage widened: in 1999 rural age-adjusted death rates were 7% higher than urban rates, and by 2019 they were 20% higher.  

4. Factor-by-factor assessment

Financial: richer overall, but more unequal and asset-dependent

Real incomes are higher than in 1990 for many households, but the distribution matters. Census reported real median household income of $83,730 in 2024, roughly flat from 2023, with stronger gains at the 90th percentile than at the 10th or median.  

The larger change is that wealth became more central to life chances. Asset owners benefited from decades of home-price and equity-market gains. Nonowners did not. That means two households with similar wages can have very different quality of life depending on whether they bought a home before prices surged, inherited wealth, or invested early.

Debt is also generationally different. At age 30, Millennials had far more education debt than Boomers at the same age, and Gen X and Millennials both had much higher total debt than Boomers.  

Best-measured: income, wealth, debt.
Thin/contested: how to value public transfers, employer benefits, and unpaid household labor in “quality of life.”

Cost of living: cheap goods, expensive essentials

The CPI says overall prices rose substantially, but the composition matters more than the aggregate. Many tradable goods — electronics, clothing, basic household items — became cheaper or better in quality. But non-tradable essentials became much more burdensome: housing, healthcare, childcare, and higher education.

Food is a mixed case. USDA data show Americans spent 9.7% of disposable personal income on food in 2024, lower than 10.4% in 1997, with food-at-home falling as a share and food-away-from-home rising. This suggests food became cheaper relative to income over the long run, although the 2021–2023 inflation shock was painful.  

Healthcare is a major negative. KFF data show out-of-pocket health spending per person rose from $115 in 1970 to $1,514 in 2023, while insurance now covers a much larger share of costs. That means the system buys more care, but premiums, deductibles, employer compensation tradeoffs, and complexity all weigh on households.  

Higher education is also mixed. Sticker prices rose enormously: College Board reports 2025–26 published tuition and fees of $11,950 for in-state public four-year colleges and $45,000 for private nonprofits. But net tuition after grants is lower than sticker prices and has declined from earlier peaks at public institutions.  

Childcare is one of the clearest family-life deteriorations. Child Care Aware reports childcare prices rose 29% from 2020 to 2024, faster than overall inflation, and Census research finds single-child childcare costs can consume a large share of median family income.  

Best-measured: CPI, food share, tuition, healthcare spending.
Thin/contested: childcare history back to 1990, quality-adjusted education costs, and whether CPI fully captures free digital goods and product quality improvements.

Housing: the central dividing line

Housing is the biggest reason the answer differs by income, age, and geography. Homeowners, especially older ones, often became wealthier. Renters and first-time buyers faced worsening affordability.

JCHS describes a housing market with record renter cost burdens, rising rents, rising homelessness, and home prices increasingly out of reach relative to income. Brookings estimates the U.S. housing shortage at about 4.9 million units at the end of 2023, though shortage estimates vary widely depending on methodology.  

The national homeownership rate remains around the mid-60% range, but that aggregate masks age and timing. Older owners are often fine; young renters trying to enter the market are not.  

Best-measured: rents, prices, homeownership, cost burdens.
Contested: exact housing-shortage size, how much is zoning versus interest rates versus construction costs versus migration.

Health and longevity: medical progress, but unequal and recently disappointing

Life expectancy is higher than in 1990, but the U.S. has had a poor recent trajectory compared with peer countries. CDC reported U.S. life expectancy rose to 79.0 in 2024 from 78.4 in 2023, but this follows pandemic-era declines and longer-running working-age mortality problems.  

The income gradient is stark. Research from the Health Inequality Project found that the richest Americans live much longer than the poorest — roughly 15 years longer for men and 10 years longer for women — and that gains were much stronger at the top than the bottom in the 2001–2014 period.  

Chronic disease is a major negative. CDC reports that 129 million Americans have at least one major chronic disease, and adult obesity remains very high.  

Rural health outcomes are especially concerning. CDC finds rural residents are more likely than urban residents to die prematurely from leading causes such as heart disease, cancer, unintentional injury, chronic lower respiratory disease, and stroke.  

Best-measured: life expectancy, mortality, obesity, chronic disease.
Thin/contested: exact causal weighting among healthcare access, diet, opioids, social isolation, work conditions, and environment.

Social fabric: more freedom, less cohesion

Family formation has become later and less universal. Census and related demographic data show declining marriage rates, more cohabitation, lower fertility, and more births outside marriage compared with earlier decades. Median age at first marriage has also risen substantially.  

Trust has fallen. Pew’s work using General Social Survey data shows the share saying most people can be trusted declined from 46% in 1972 to 34% in 2018, and Pew finds trust in the federal government remains near historic lows.  

Social mobility also looks weaker than the postwar ideal. Opportunity Insights research found absolute mobility — the share of children earning more than their parents — fell from around 90% for the 1940 birth cohort to about 50% for children born in the 1980s.  

This is not all negative. People have more freedom to leave bad marriages, choose different family structures, migrate, work remotely, find niche communities, and access information. But the older package of marriage, local institutions, churches, unions, civic clubs, stable employers, and affordable starter homes weakened.

Best-measured: marriage, fertility, trust surveys, mobility.
Thin/contested: whether lower marriage is “worse” versus more individual freedom; how to measure friendship, loneliness, and community quality consistently since 1990.

Technology: enormous convenience, ambiguous human effects

Technology is the clearest improvement in day-to-day convenience. Compared with 1990, Americans have instant navigation, online banking, remote work, telemedicine, e-commerce, cheap entertainment, better cars, better logistics, and access to nearly unlimited information.

It also improved productivity, though unevenly. BLS data show labor productivity growth accelerated in some periods, especially the late 1990s and recently, though long-run productivity growth since the mid-2000s has been slower than the late-1990s boom.  

The labor-market downside is real. Research by Acemoglu and Restrepo links automation and task displacement to major changes in wage structure over recent decades, especially for routine-task workers.  

The attention and mental-health evidence is contested. The Surgeon General has warned that the evidence is not sufficient to conclude social media is safe for young people, but some research finds daily social media use is not consistently associated with depressive symptoms. That makes causality much less settled than the raw mental-health trend.  

Best-measured: adoption, productivity, online access, some labor displacement.
Thin/contested: how to price free digital goods, how much social media causes mental-health deterioration, and whether remote work improves or weakens social life.

Summary verdict by group

Income

* Bottom quintile: materially better in goods, public health coverage, and technology; worse or precarious in housing, wealth, health, and stability. Verdict: mixed-to-worse.
* Second quintile: more consumption ability than 1990, but squeezed by essentials. Verdict: mixed.
* Middle quintile: better technology and real income, but the traditional middle-class path is harder. Verdict: mixed.
* Fourth quintile: generally better, especially if homeowners. Verdict: moderately better.
* Top quintile: much better financially and technologically. Verdict: better, with social/mental caveats.

Generation

* Boomers: best outcome if they owned homes and retirement assets; weaker for renters or those with poor health. Verdict: mostly better.
* Gen X: recovered from major shocks, but unevenly. Verdict: mixed-positive.
* Millennials: worse early adulthood, improving wealth for some, still housing-constrained. Verdict: bifurcated.
* Gen Z: high convenience and opportunity, but high housing costs and mental-health stress. Verdict: mixed, leaning strained.

Geography

* Urban: high opportunity, high costs. Verdict: better for high earners, worse for many renters.
* Suburban: strong wealth and family-life gains for owners; hard entry for young families. Verdict: mostly better for incumbents.
* Rural: lower housing prices but weaker health, income, and access trends. Verdict: mixed-to-worse.

The biggest tensions

1. Richer but less secure.
    Real incomes, wealth, and technology improved, but security became more dependent on asset ownership, employer benefits, and geography.
2. Cheaper goods, pricier essentials.
    TVs, software, information, and many goods became vastly better or cheaper. Housing, healthcare, childcare, and education became the binding constraints.
3. More freedom, weaker institutions.
    People have more individual choice and access to information, but family formation, institutional trust, local community, and shared civic life look weaker.

Uncertainty and weighting bias

My weighting puts heavy emphasis on housing, health, family formation, and wealth security, because those determine whether people can build stable lives. A more consumption-focused analysis would look more positive because modern goods, services, medicine, safety technology, and digital tools are dramatically better than in 1990. A more social-conservative or community-focused analysis would look more negative because marriage, trust, fertility, and institutional participation have weakened.

The evidence is strongest for income, wealth, debt, housing, life expectancy, mortality, and prices. It is weaker for loneliness, attention, meaning, family quality, community strength, and the value of free digital technology. The hardest tradeoff is that many gains are real but unevenly distributed, while many losses are broad but difficult to price.
