This is a big one — let me pull current data on a few key trend lines before synthesizing.

Here's my synthesis, combining current data with the well-established 1990–present research record. I'll flag where I'm drawing on training knowledge vs. fresh sources, and where evidence is genuinely contested.

## 1. Financial

**The topline is better than the vibes suggest, but gains are back-loaded and unequal.** Real median household income rose from roughly $63K (1990, in today's dollars) to ~$84,000 in 2024  — but nearly all of that came in two bursts: 1994–1999 and 2015–2019, with long stagnant stretches between. By quintile: CBO and Census data show the top quintile captured most gains — the Gini coefficient rose from 0.43 in 1990 to 0.49 in 2024,  and the mean income of the bottom quintile was ~$18,500 in 2024 vs. ~$560,000 for the top 5%, with the gap widening since the 1990s. 

Important nuance often missed: the *bottom* quintile saw unusually strong real wage growth from ~2015–2023 (tight labor markets, minimum wage increases) — EPI and Autor et al.'s "wage compression" work document the 10th percentile outpacing the median post-pandemic. And CBO's *post-tax-and-transfer* series shows bottom-quintile income up substantially more than market income alone. So "the poor got nothing" is wrong; "the rich got dramatically more" is right.

**Wealth and debt** cut generationally: Fed SCF data show median wealth of under-35 households roughly flat in real terms from 1989–2019, then jumping sharply 2019–2022 (the biggest young-household wealth gain on record). Student debt went from a rounding error in 1990 to ~$1.7T. Contested: whether Millennials are permanently behind or "catching up late" — post-2020 data increasingly supports catch-up on wealth, not on housing.

## 2. Cost of living

The defining pattern is **price divergence by category** (Mark Perry's "chart of the century," from BLS CPI components since ~2000, and it extends back to 1990): TVs, toys, clothing, software down 50–95% in real terms; food roughly tracking overall CPI; but college tuition up ~170%+, hospital services up ~200%+, and childcare up ~2x relative to wages. Things you *buy* got cheap; things that gate middle-class life — housing, education, healthcare, childcare — got expensive. This hits quintiles asymmetrically: the bottom quintile spends a far larger income share on the inflated categories, which is why "CPI-adjusted incomes rose" understates felt strain for lower-income and young families. (Contested: some economists argue quality adjustment overstates healthcare/education inflation; others argue official CPI *understates* housing inflation for new entrants because it averages in long-tenured owners.)

## 3. Housing

This is the clearest deterioration, especially post-2020:

- A record 22.6 million renters (50%) are cost-burdened,  and the median existing home hit $412,500 — five times median household income,  vs. ~3x historically. The typical first-time-buyer mortgage payment is 40% higher in real terms than in 1990, requiring ~$127K income — only 6 million of 46 million renters qualify. 
- Units renting under $1,000/month fell more than 30% from 2013–2023 while units at $2,000+ nearly tripled. 
- Generationally: 38.3% of 28-year-old Gen Zers owned homes in 2025 vs. 42.5% of Gen X and 44.4% of Boomers at 28;  62% of Millennial 40-year-olds owned vs. 69% of Boomers at the same age.  Median first-time buyer age hit 40 in 2025  — it was ~29 in the early 1990s (NAR).

Supply constraints are the consensus driver (zoning, construction productivity stagnation, post-2008 underbuilding). Geographically this is the story of the era: coastal metros became wealth machines for incumbents and walls for entrants; much of the Midwest/South stayed affordable, which is where migration is flowing.

## 4. Health & longevity

**Mixed, with a shameful middle chapter and a recent rebound.** Life expectancy: 75.4 years in 1990 → 79.0 in 2024, a record high  — but that's only +3.6 years over 34 years, while peer countries average 82.7, a 3.7-year gap  that barely existed in 1990. The 2010s were nearly flat even *before* COVID, driven by opioids (Case–Deaton's deaths of despair, concentrated among non-college adults) — overdose deaths went from ~9K/year in 1990 to a 110K+ peak in 2022–23, now falling fast.

Chronic disease worsened: adult obesity ~23% (early-90s NHANES) → ~40%; diabetes prevalence roughly tripled. Youth mental health deteriorated sharply after ~2011 (CDC YRBS: persistent sadness among teen girls hit 57% in 2021; teen suicide rates up ~60% from 2007). Whether smartphones/social media are the primary cause (Haidt/Twenge) or one factor among several (Odgers and other skeptics) is genuinely contested — the correlational evidence is strong, causal evidence weaker.

Geographically: life expectancy declined in rural America while increasing in urban America  — a rural-urban mortality gap that was small in 1990 is now roughly 2+ years and driven by higher rural rates for all top-10 causes of death,  plus hospital closures. This maps tightly onto education and income, so the geography and class stories are the same story.

## 5. Social fabric

Mostly worse, on nearly every measurable dimension — this is where I have the least confidence the metrics capture what matters, but the direction is consistent:

- **Family formation**: TFR ~2.08 (1990) → ~1.6; median first marriage age up ~4 years; share of 25–34-year-olds married down from ~60%+ to under 40%. Marriage increasingly stratified by class — stable for college graduates, collapsing below.
- **Trust**: Gallup shows confidence in most major institutions at or near record lows; General Social Survey interpersonal trust ("most people can be trusted") fell from ~40% to low 30s. Putnam documented the associational decline through 2000; it continued. Time-use data (Kraut, then the Surgeon General's 2023 loneliness advisory) show in-person socializing down sharply, especially for young men.
- **Mobility**: Chetty's landmark finding — ~90% of children born in 1940 out-earned their parents vs. ~50% of those born in the mid-1980s. *Relative* mobility (rank-to-rank) has been roughly flat; it's *absolute* mobility that halved, mostly because growth slowed and concentrated at the top.

## 6. Technology

The genuinely universal improvement — and the hardest to price. Communication, information, entertainment, navigation, and logistics that would have cost fortunes in 1990 are near-free; GDP largely misses this (Brynjolfsson's consumer-surplus studies estimate people demand thousands of dollars/year to give up search or maps). It's also the most *egalitarian* gain: the bottom quintile got the same smartphone as the top.

Downsides: measured productivity growth actually *slowed* post-2005 despite the tech boom (the Solow paradox redux); average screen time is ~7 hours/day; attention, sleep, and adolescent wellbeing costs are real but contested in magnitude; and AI-driven labor displacement is now a live question — early evidence (2024–25 studies on entry-level hiring in exposed occupations) is suggestive but thin. I'd flag my own position here: as an AI, I'm plausibly biased toward optimism about this category.

## Verdicts by group

**By quintile:** Top quintile — *clearly better* (income, wealth, longevity, and tech gains all compounding; college-educated life expectancy kept rising even through the 2010s). Middle quintiles — *mixed*: real incomes modestly up, goods cheap, but the housing/education/childcare gauntlet consumes the gains and the path to ownership narrowed. Bottom quintile — *genuinely mixed, not simply worse*: material consumption, post-transfer income, and recent wage growth are all up, but housing burden is crushing (70%+ cost-burden rates for renters earning $30–45K),  and health/social outcomes deteriorated most here.

**By generation:** Boomers — *best of the deal*: bought assets cheap, sold dear, and now hold outsized wealth with ~80% homeownership.  Gen X — *better, with a caveat*: hit hardest by 2008 in mid-career but largely recovered and now hold peak-earning-years wealth. Millennials — *worse on the traditional ladder, better lately*: behind Boomers on ownership and family formation at every age, but wealth has caught up faster post-2020 than the "poorest generation" narrative allows. Gen Z — *too early, diverging internally*: early cohorts tracked at or near Gen X ownership rates at 25,  but they face the worst affordability math and the worst measured mental health of any cohort at the same age.

**By geography:** Urban — *better economically, worse on affordability*: the knowledge economy concentrated wages, amenities, and longevity gains in metros, but housing costs captured much of the surplus, and entry-level urban life got much harder. Suburban — *probably the best aggregate outcome*: captured metro labor markets plus (until recently) affordable ownership; now absorbing the affordability spillover. Rural — *clearly worse*: relative income decline, hospital and institution loss, population loss in about half of counties, and the mortality divergence above. If one group unambiguously lost since 1990, it's non-college rural America.

## The biggest tensions

1. **Cheaper goods, pricier foundations.** Consumption abundance rose while the assets and services that constitute middle-class security — a home, a degree, care for kids and elders — inflated past wages. This single tension explains most of the gap between GDP statistics and public mood.
1. **Longer-lived (again) but sicker and sadder.** Mortality is at a record best, yet chronic disease, adolescent mental illness, and loneliness are at or near record worsts. We got better at not dying and worse at flourishing.
1. **Connected but atomized.** Infinite low-cost communication coincided with declining marriage, friendship, trust, and community — the technology that was supposed to bind us correlates with (and may partly cause) the thinning of social fabric.

## My uncertainty and bias

Three honest caveats. First, **weighting is unavoidable and value-laden**: there's no principled exchange rate between "smartphone access" and "can't buy a house" or between +3.6 years of life and a halved chance of out-earning your parents. Someone who weights community and family formation heavily will read this record as decline; someone who weights consumption, information access, and (recent) longevity will read it as progress. Second, **measurement asymmetry**: economic and mortality data are excellent; social-fabric and wellbeing data rely on surveys with changing instruments, so I'm more confident in the *direction* of social decline than its magnitude — and CPI disputes could move the real-income story a few points either way. Third, **my own priors**: I likely over-trust quantified national datasets relative to lived experience, and being an AI, I should be suspected of underweighting technology's costs. I've tried to correct for both, but flag them.
